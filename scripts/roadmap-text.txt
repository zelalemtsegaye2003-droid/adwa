1. Executive Summary

This roadmap converts the Adwa Cyber startup proposal (v1.0, May 2026)
into an executable, fundable programme — and it deliberately
re-baselines the plan: the platform descriptions in the proposal are
treated as design specifications rather than existing assets, and Phase
1, the compliance-automation platform, is scheduled to be built from
scratch in Months 1–6. The verdict on the proposal is positive: it is
technically credible, methodologically rigorous, and unusually well
aligned with Ethiopia's policy architecture — the National Cybersecurity
Policy (NCSP) 2024, Digital Ethiopia 2030, and Proclamation No.
1321/2024. What the venture now needs is both working software and
execution scaffolding: a legal entity, a production-grade platform,
signed pilot commitments, a team, a budget, and a funding strategy. This
document supplies the plan for all of it.

The central strategic recommendation is to re-sequence the five phases
into three delivery tracks. Track A (Months 1–12) builds Phase 1
(compliance automation) from scratch in Months 1–6, productises it as
subscription software, and stands up Phase 2 (CERT-as-a-Service) on the
same open-source infrastructure in the second half of the year — because
together they address acute, penalised pain: manual audits of 40–80
hours against 47 legal obligations, with penalties of up to 4 percent of
global turnover and executive imprisonment, and therefore can be sold,
not just piloted. Track B runs Phase 3 (multilingual awareness) in
parallel as a grant-funded mission programme, developing its Amharic
curriculum and training corpus during the first half of the year. Track
C defers Phases 4 and 5 until partnership, authorisation, and
human-rights gates are passed, protecting the venture's credibility and
donor eligibility.

The 12-month roadmap follows the proposal's own Design Science Research
calendar: legal setup, rule formalisation, architecture, and signed
letters of intent in Quarter 1; the Phase 1 build to MVP and production
deployment on Ethiopian hosting in Quarter 2; paid pilots with the
Commercial Bank of Ethiopia, Dashen Bank, and three microfinance
institutions from Month 6; pilot execution, SOC stand-up, and the
five-school awareness pilot in Quarter 3; and evaluation, GCI 2026
questionnaire data, open-sourcing of the Compliance API, and a seed
round in Quarter 4. The Year 1 budget is estimated at USD
104,000–193,000 (indicative planning range), funded by a deliberate
hybrid: development-partner grants, university in-kind support, and paid
pilot revenue — with equity investment deferred until pilot metrics
justify a fair valuation.

The five highest-priority actions, developed in Sections 2–14, are: (1)
start the Phase 1 from-scratch build immediately — rule catalogue in
Month 1, MVP and production deployment by Month 6; (2) convert every
“Planned” pilot into a signed letter of intent before making any
external claim; (3) establish the legal entity and a technology-transfer
agreement with Bahir Dar University; (4) re-sequence delivery into the
three tracks described above; and (5) charge for pilots from day one.
Executed together, these steps turn a strong research artefact into an
investable company while preserving the proposal's national mission —
accelerating Ethiopia's trajectory from GCI Tier 3 toward Tier 2 by
2028.

2. Strategic Assessment of the Proposal

2.1 What the Proposal Gets Right

Four strengths distinguish this proposal from typical startup documents
and should be preserved in every future version. First, policy alignment
is concrete, not rhetorical: every phase maps to a specific NCSP
section, and Appendix A of the proposal traces each NCSP tactic to a
product component. This makes the venture legible to INSA, the Ministry
of Innovation and Technology (MInT), and international assessors.
Second, the evidence base is current and specific — INTERPOL's Africa
Cyberthreat Assessment 2025 (which places Ethiopia first globally for
malware detections in 2024), the ITU Global Cybersecurity Index 2024
(Tier 3), and INSA's report of 27,773 sophisticated attacks in a single
six-month period in 2025–2026. Third, the Design Science Research
methodology with six defined research questions and quantitative success
thresholds (for example, greater than 70 percent audit-time reduction;
detection-to-notification latency under 15 minutes) gives funders a
built-in evaluation framework. Fourth, the technical design is
build-ready: the 47 obligations of Proclamation 1321/2024 are
inventoried with enough precision to be formalised as machine-readable
rules, and the specified open-source stack (Wazuh, MISP, BERT variants)
is mature, licence-clean, and well matched to a small team building from
scratch — which is exactly how this roadmap schedules the build.

The same document, submitted to a bank procurement committee or a
development-partner review panel today, would face avoidable objections.
The gaps below are all fixable within 90 days, and the roadmap in

2.3 Assessment Conclusion

The proposal is a strong research and vision document that now needs a
commercial and institutional wrapper. The technical design needs no
rethinking; what must be added is its construction and everything around
it: a company that can sign contracts, a platform built from scratch and
deployed to production, paying customers who validate the value
proposition, and a governance layer that protects both the founder and
the university. The remainder of this roadmap is organised around
exactly that addition. It is also worth stating plainly: the sequencing,
budget, and revenue figures in this document are planning instruments
based on the proposal's own data and clearly labelled market estimates —
they are decision aids, not forecasts, and should be re-validated as
pilot data arrives.

3. Implementation Sequencing Strategy

3.1 Why the Linear Sequence Should Change

The proposal presents the five phases as a single integrated programme,
with Phases 4 and 5 (content filtering and critical-infrastructure
protection) scheduled alongside the rest. That framing is appropriate
for a national-policy audience but hazardous for a startup. Phases 4 and
5 depend on partnerships that do not yet exist (the Ethiopian Media
Authority, four CII operators), on authorisation pathways that must be
confirmed with INSA and legal counsel, and — in the case of Phase 4 — on
navigating Ethiopia's most politically sensitive terrain, where a
mis-step could damage the credibility of the entire platform and its
university affiliation. Pursuing all five phases simultaneously would
also dilute a small team across five codebases and five sales motions
before a single customer has paid or a single line of production code
has been written.

The recommended structure is therefore three tracks with explicit gates.
The tracks differ in funding source, management rhythm, and risk
profile, which is precisely what makes the programme manageable: the
commercial track is judged by delivery milestones and revenue, the
mission track by reach and learning outcomes, and the deferred track by
gate criteria rather than dates.

3.2 The Three Tracks

-   Track A — Commercial core (Phases 1 and 2). Build Phase 1 from
    scratch in Months 1–6 (Section 4.1): the rule catalogue, the
    compliance engine, the scan engine, and the web, executive, and
    Amharic dashboards on the open-source stack. Productise it as
    subscription software, then stand up the CERT capability (Phase 2)
    as a managed service on the same infrastructure, with 24/7 coverage
    phased in from late Year 1 into Year 2. These phases solve acute,
    quantified pain: a manual compliance audit takes 40–80 hours of
    specialist work against obligations that carry penalties of up to 4
    percent of global turnover and imprisonment, and private-sector
    incident response currently does not exist. Pain that is penalised
    and quantified is pain that budgets can be found for. Track A is the
    only track expected to generate revenue in Year 1.

-   Track B — Mission programme (Phase 3). The multilingual awareness
    platform serves school children and the public interest, not paying
    customers, and should be funded accordingly: by education and
    digital-inclusion grants, in partnership with Bahir Dar University
    and the Ministry of Education. Its Amharic curriculum and training
    corpus are developed within the track during the first half of Year
    1 by a grant-funded education specialist rather than engineers, so
    the five-school pilot can run in Months 7–9 in parallel, without
    competing with Track A for engineering attention.

-   Track C — Gated expansion (Phases 4 and 5). Content filtering and
    CII protection are strategically important but institutionally
    demanding. They should advance only through the gates in Table 2 —
    partnership commitments, legal authorisation, human-rights due
    diligence, and dedicated funding — so that the core business is
    never hostage to their slower institutional clocks.

3.3 What the Tracks Preserve

This sequencing does not retreat from the proposal's ambition — it
protects it. Every phase remains on the roadmap, the DSR research
questions remain the evaluation spine, and the national-mission
narrative (indigenous AI, digital sovereignty, the Adwa name) remains
the brand. What changes is order and ownership: the venture earns the
right to build Phases 4 and 5 by first building and proving Phases 1 and
2 with paying customers, which is exactly the evidence INSA, the EMA,
and CII operators will ask for anyway. In negotiations with state
institutions, a startup that says “we protect banks today, and here is
our certified, audited platform” is far stronger than one that asks for
sensitive mandates on the strength of a proposal alone.

4. Twelve-Month Execution Roadmap (Year 1)

The Year 1 calendar below is deliberately aligned with the Design
Science Research timeline committed to in the proposal — demonstration
in Months 5–9 and evaluation in Months 10–12 — so that the commercial
programme and the research programme reinforce rather than compete with
each other. Because Phase 1 is built from scratch, the first half of the
year belongs to the build: the MVP is complete and in production by the
end of Month 6, pilots onboard from Month 6, and the demonstration
window is satisfied by pilot onboarding and early execution. Each
quarter has one dominant objective; everything else is subordinate to
it.

4.1 The Phase 1 Build Plan (Months 1–6)

Building the compliance-automation platform from scratch in six months
is the critical path of the entire roadmap, and it is planned as five
workstreams with hard exit criteria. “From scratch” means exactly that:
no existing codebase is assumed, and every component — rule catalogue,
engines, dashboards, deployment — is specified, built, tested, and
hardened within the window. The proposal's design (the 47-obligation
inventory, the open-source stack, the dashboard concepts) is the
blueprint; the build turns it into a running system.

Two disciplines protect the schedule. Scope: the MVP is defined tightly
— rule engine, scan engine, and the three dashboards — while API
integrations (including Ethio-CERT synchronisation) and advanced
analytics are post-MVP enhancements scheduled only after pilot feedback.
Team: the build is staffed by the CTO (hired Month 1–2), a full-stack
engineer (Month 3–4), and the founder, using mature open-source
components instead of custom foundations; if two consecutive sprint
milestones slip, the response is to descope, not to extend. The build
window deliberately overlaps the pilot-contracting window: customers
sign in Months 4–5 against the MVP scope, so onboarding starts the
moment acceptance tests pass.

4.2 Quarter 1 (Months 1–3): Foundation and Build Kick-off

The first quarter removes every credibility gap identified in Section 2
and starts the build. The company is registered as a private limited
company and a technology-transfer agreement is negotiated with Bahir Dar
University (Section 6). The Phase 1 build kicks off with the
rule-formalisation workstream — the 47 obligations converted into
machine-readable rules with counsel validation — and the architecture
blueprint is finalised (Section 4.1). The CTO is hired in Month 1–2, and
the development environment, repositories, and CI pipeline go live. In
parallel, the founder converts pilot interest into signed letters of
intent, starting with the organisations the proposal names: CBE, Dashen
Bank, the Wasasa, Oromia, and Amhara MFIs, and the five e-commerce,
logistics, and fintech SMEs. Track B begins curriculum development with
a grant-funded education specialist, and the first grant applications
are drafted.

4.3 Quarter 2 (Months 4–6): Build to MVP and First Paid Pilots

Quarter 2 is dominated by the core build sprints and opens the
demonstration phase of the DSR cycle. The full-stack engineer joins; the
rule engine, scan engine, and dashboards come together sprint by sprint;
and the platform is hardened and deployed to production on Ethiopian
hosting with a public domain, TLS encryption, and monitoring — a
precondition for any bank evaluation. Pilot contracts are signed in
Months 4–5 against the MVP scope, each structured as a six-month
engagement with defined success metrics drawn from the proposal's
research questions (audit-time reduction above 70 percent;
detection-to-notification latency below 15 minutes; platform uptime
above 99.5 percent), with onboarding beginning the moment MVP acceptance
passes in Month 6. An ISO 27001 certification timetable is agreed with a
shortlisted assessor, and the first grant applications for Track B and
for working capital are submitted (Section 9).

4.4 Quarter 3 (Months 7–9): Pilot Execution and SOC Stand-up

Pilot execution dominates the quarter: metrics are collected
continuously against the RQ thresholds, and monthly steering-committee
reviews with each pilot customer build the reference relationships that
Quarter 4 will convert into case studies. The SOC stands up on the same
open-source infrastructure: the SOC manager and analysts hired this
quarter deploy Wazuh-based monitoring and the MISP threat-intelligence
feed for pilot customers, beginning with business-hours coverage and
on-call escalation, while full 24/7 coverage is a Year 2 objective. In
parallel, the Phase 3 school pilot with five Addis Ababa public schools
runs on grant funding, using the curriculum completed in the first half
of the year. Track C groundwork proceeds quietly: a human-rights
due-diligence scoping paper for Phase 4 is drafted with EHRC engagement,
and technical MOU conversations continue with CII operators without any
public claims.

4.5 Quarter 4 (Months 10–12): Evaluation, Visibility, and Capital

The evaluation phase closes the DSR cycle. Pilot results are analysed
(paired t-tests for audit-time reduction, latency distribution analysis
for SOC performance, as specified in the proposal's evaluation table)
and written into two bank-grade case studies and at least one
peer-reviewed submission. The Adwa Cyber data feed is prepared for the
GCI 2026 national questionnaire, in coordination with MInT, as the
proposal's next-steps table plans. The Compliance API is open-sourced on
GitHub — a deliberate credibility and talent-attraction move committed
to in the proposal. The 24/7 SOC transition plan for Year 2 is finalised
with the SOC manager. Finally, a seed funding round opens, supported by
pilot metrics, LOIs converted to annual contracts, and the ISO 27001
programme underway.

5. Years 2–3 Scale-Up Plan (2028–2029)

Years 2 and 3 shift the company from proving to scaling. The organising
logic is simple: Year 1 builds the product from scratch and proves it
with paid pilots; Year 2 productises and repeatably sells it; Year 3
embeds it nationally and opens the regional option. The targets below
extend the proposal's own KPI table (500 organisations using compliance
tools by 2027; five operational sectoral CERTs; 20 percent school
coverage) to the company level.

5.1 Year 2 (2028): Productise and Sell

The pilot relationships convert into annual subscriptions, and the sales
motion becomes repeatable: a standardised compliance scan as the
lead-generation offer, a fixed-price onboarding package, and annual
contracts with defined service levels for the CERT service. The 24/7
SOC, whose staff and runbooks were built in the second half of Year 1,
becomes fully operational in the first half of Year 2, converting
pilot-period business-hours monitoring into a round-the-clock managed
service. ISO 27001 certification is completed — the audit cycle that
began in Year 1 concludes here — unlocking larger bank and government
contracts that require certified suppliers. The Phase 3 platform,
validated in the school pilot, scales toward the proposal's target of 20
percent of target schools, funded by the education grants secured in
Year 1. Team size roughly doubles to 18–22. Track C gates are formally
reviewed at mid-year: if the EMA partnership and human-rights due
diligence for Phase 4 are complete, a limited Phase 4 deployment with
the three media organisations named in the proposal (Fanaka, Ethio
Forum, Sheger FM) may proceed; if not, it slips without any impact on
the core business.

5.2 Year 3 (2029): National Footprint and Regional Option

Year 3 pursues three objectives. First, national coverage of the
commercial core: the compliance subscriber base grows toward the
proposal's 2030 trajectory (2,000+ organisations), and CERT-as-a-Service
expands from banking into telecom and energy, in direct support of the
sectoral-CERT count the proposal targets (20+ by 2030). Second, if the
Phase 5 gates are passed — at least two CII operator MOUs, confirmed
authorisation, IEC 62443-aligned design — the CII pilot with the four
operators named in the proposal (Ethiopian Electric Power, Ethiopian
Airlines, Ethio Telecom, CBE) begins under dedicated institutional
funding with liability insurance in place. Third, the regional
replication study recommended in the proposal's own limitations section
(Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda) is commissioned with a regional development
partner, since the architecture was deliberately designed for
replication.

5.3 Scale-Up Guardrails

Three disciplines should govern the scale-up. Revenue concentration must
be managed: no single client should exceed roughly 30 percent of revenue
by the end of Year 2, which argues for prioritising breadth in the SME
segment alongside the anchor banks. Hiring must lag validated demand
rather than lead projections — the Year 2 plan above assumes conversion
rates, not pipeline hope. And every expansion (new sector, new phase,
new country) must pass a mini-gate modelled on Section 3.2: a named
partner, a funding source, and a defined kill criterion. Companies die
more often from unfocused expansion than from slow growth, and a venture
carrying a national-security mission has an additional obligation to
scale only where it can deliver.

6. Legal, Governance and Institutional Setup

6.1 Company Formation

Adwa Cyber currently exists as a research lab affiliation, not a
contracting entity. No bank can sign a paid pilot with, and no grant can
be disbursed to, an unregistered activity. The first executive action of
the roadmap is therefore to register a private limited company (PLC) at
the Ethiopian Commercial Register, with the founder as initial
shareholder. Registration should be executed with a commercial lawyer
familiar with technology ventures; as an indicative planning range,
incorporation and initial counsel typically cost USD 1,500–4,000 and
complete within two to six weeks, though both figures must be validated
locally. The company should from the outset maintain a clean IP ledger:
everything developed before incorporation is assigned to the company in
writing, and everything after is developed under contracts that so
provide.

6.2 The Bahir Dar University Relationship

The proposal invites the university to be the academic home of the
initiative — providing research oversight, pilot facilitation, and
institutional credibility. That relationship is an asset worth
formalising precisely because it is valuable to both sides. The
recommended instrument is a technology-transfer and affiliation
agreement covering four subjects: ownership and licensing of
intellectual property developed in the lab (a clean default is
university ownership of pre-existing academic work, company ownership of
new product code, with a cross-licence and, if the university prefers, a
small royalty or equity position); the terms under which students and
faculty may work on company projects, including the internship pipeline
in Section 7; the use of the university's name and facilities; and the
ethical-review pathway for research involving organisations and minors,
which the proposal already commits to. Universities increasingly
standardise such agreements; approaching BDU's research and
technology-transfer office early, with a draft rather than a request,
will shorten the negotiation.

6.3 Regulatory Engagement: INSA and Ethio-CERT

Cybersecurity services, threat-intelligence sharing, and especially any
monitoring of networks touch regulated territory. The proposal assumes
deep technical integration with Ethio-CERT (API synchronisation is a
planned post-MVP enhancement in the Phase 2 build) and an observation
role for INSA in the pilot design — both are the right instincts. The
roadmap adds a formal step: before selling monitoring services beyond
the pilot scope, the company should obtain written confirmation from
INSA of the authorisation or licensing pathway applicable to private
cybersecurity service providers, and structure its data-handling
(metadata-only audits, privacy-preserving sharing protocols,
Ethiopian-soil storage under Article 22 of Proclamation 1321/2024) to
match. The specific permits, if any, required for a private SOC
operating in Ethiopia should be confirmed with counsel rather than
assumed; this item is flagged as requiring verification in the risk
register.

6.4 Governance Structure

A startup selling security services to banks will be judged on its
governance as much as its code. Three lightweight structures are
recommended for Year 1. First, an advisory board of five to seven
members meeting quarterly: a senior BDU faculty member (academic
oversight), a former bank or telecom CISO (credibility with buyers), an
INSA or Ethio-CERT liaison as permitted (regulatory alignment), a lawyer
with technology and data-protection practice, and one international
cyber-capacity expert (useful for grant applications). Second, a pilot
steering committee per pilot customer, meeting monthly — this doubles as
the customer-success motion. Third, a founder-level discipline of
written board minutes and decisions, which costs little now and is
indispensable at the first investment round. Together these structures
also begin to address the key-person risk flagged in Section 2.2.

7.1 Retention and Key-Person De-Risking

Cybersecurity talent is scarce and mobile everywhere, and Ethiopia is no
exception. Three retention levers are recommended: certification
sponsorship (ISO 27001 lead auditor, and a structured path toward
CISSP-equivalent seniority) which costs little and signals investment in
the individual; compensation positioned at or slightly above the 60th
percentile of the local market with annual review; and an employee share
option pool of 10–15 percent established at incorporation, vesting over
four years — inexpensive now, and standard diligence hygiene for the
seed round. Key-person risk around the founder is addressed structurally
in Year 1: the CTO owns the architecture, all systems and decisions are
documented, the advisory board meets quarterly, and the BDU agreement
guarantees continuity of the academic relationship independently of any
individual.

8.2 Revenue Model

Three revenue lines match the three tracks. The compliance platform
(Phase 1) sells as an annual subscription tiered by organisation size,
anchored against the alternative: a manual audit costing 40–80

from the outset: price in USD or with an annual birr adjustment clause
(multi-year contracts signed in a depreciating currency otherwise

against the annual list price, not free pilots, which erode both revenue

10. Go-to-Market and Partnership Strategy

10.1 Beachhead: The Financial Sector

The beachhead is the segment where pain, budget, and regulatory
attention intersect: banks and microfinance institutions. The wedge
message writes itself from the proposal's own evidence — the Personal
Data Protection Proclamation's 47 obligations, penalties of up to 4
percent of global turnover and imprisonment, the 72-hour
breach-notification clock, and manual audits that take 40–80 hours. For
a bank CISO, the pitch is concrete: an Ethiopian-law-native compliance
engine and a 24/7 monitoring service at a fraction of the cost of
building either in-house or importing a foreign tool that does not map
to Proclamation 1321/2024. The Commercial Bank of Ethiopia and Dashen
Bank are already named pilot candidates; the microfinance institutions
(Wasasa, Oromia, Amhara) extend the beachhead into a segment with less
in-house capability and more price sensitivity, ideal for the
standardised offering.

10.2 The Sales Motion

The motion from first contact to contract has five steps, each with an
exit criterion. One: an anchored introduction through INSA, Ethio-CERT,
BDU, or a peer CISO — warm channels already implicit in the proposal's
design. Two: a free, scoped compliance scan against a subset of the 47
rules, producing a findings report the prospect keeps — the single most
persuasive artifact the platform can generate, and a natural
productisation of the Phase 1 engine once the MVP is live. Three: a
six-month paid pilot with metrics agreed up front (the proposal's pilot
design already specifies participants, phases, and durations). Four: a
steering-committee rhythm during the pilot that builds the internal
champions who will defend renewal. Five: conversion to an annual
contract at pilot pricing plus a defined step. Free pilots are
explicitly rejected: they signal low value, attract non-committed
participants, and consume the same delivery capacity as paid ones.

10.3 Partnership Architecture

Four partnership tiers carry the go-to-market. Bahir Dar University
provides the academic legitimacy and the student pipeline; its name
opens ministry and school-system doors that a startup alone cannot. INSA
and Ethio-CERT provide the regulatory integration and, over time, the
endorsement without which security sales to state-adjacent buyers stall
— the planned Ethio-CERT API synchronisation (a Phase 2 build
deliverable) is the intended technical foundation of this tier. The
anchor banks are design partners whose case studies become the reference
assets for the wider financial sector. Hosting and telecom partners
(Ethio Telecom, Ethiopian cloud providers) supply the sovereign
infrastructure the data-residency rules require, and can become channel
partners reselling compliance scanning to their enterprise customers.
Phase 4 and 5 partnerships — the EMA, the CII operators — belong to
Track C and follow its gates; they are cultivated quietly through Year 1
but carry no revenue expectations.

10.4 Competitive Positioning

Against international vendors, Adwa Cyber's position is not cheaper
security but native security: law-native (47 obligations of the actual
proclamation encoded as rules, not GDPR mappings), language-native
(Amharic dashboards and curriculum), and sovereignty-native
(Ethiopian-soil hosting, indigenous IP — the exact argument the
proposal's discussion section makes). Against local consultancies, the
position is product over person-days: repeatable software and a 24/7
service rather than audit reports that age. This positioning should be
stated consistently in every artifact — website, case studies, grant
applications — and is the brand answer to the question every buyer and
funder will ask: why not buy foreign? The proposal has already written
the answer; the go-to-market's job is to prove it with paying customers.

11.1 The Four Risks That Matter Most

Phase 1 delivery (R1) is the risk the whole roadmap hangs on. Encoding
47 legal obligations into a working engine — with three dashboards and a
production deployment — inside six months is achievable with the
open-source stack and a focused team, but only with ruthless scope
discipline. The roadmap absorbs slippage three ways: the MVP is defined
tightly and everything else is post-MVP; pilot contracts are signed
against the MVP scope, so a slipped enhancement never delays a pilot;
and the sprint cadence surfaces trouble every two weeks, early enough to
descope rather than extend. The build is also timed so that MVP
acceptance at the end of Month 6 lands inside the DSR demonstration
window (Months 5–9), which pilot onboarding and early execution satisfy.

Sales-cycle length (R2) is the risk most likely to bend the roadmap even
when the build goes well. Ethiopian financial institutions buy
deliberately, and a six-month pilot can sit behind three months of
procurement. The roadmap absorbs this by starting LOI conversion in
Month 1, by keeping the initial paid engagement small enough to be
approved by a single executive, and by never letting the pipeline rest
on one institution — the MFI and SME segments exist in the plan
precisely to provide faster-moving counterparties.

Key-person risk (R6) is the risk investors will price hardest. A single
founder holding all relationships, all architecture knowledge, and all
fundraising context is a discount on any term sheet. Every structural
mitigation in this roadmap — the CTO hire in Quarter 1, the
documentation discipline, the advisory board, the university agreement —
is also, deliberately, an investor-readiness measure.

Phase 4 reputational adjacency (R11) is the risk with the longest
shadow. Content filtering in any country sits next to censorship, and
for a venture whose credibility rests on a university partnership and
development funding, being framed as a surveillance vendor would be
disproportionately damaging. The Track C gates exist to make the
venture's position unambiguous: no deployment without the EMA
partnership, the EHRC-engaged due-diligence report, and the transparency
framework the proposal itself designed — appeal tiers, judicial
oversight, quarterly independent audit, and 90-day data retention
limits, all adopted verbatim.

11.2 Early-Warning Indicators

Five indicators should be watched monthly and trigger action when
crossed: no signed LOI by the end of Month 3 (sales-cycle risk
materialising — escalate partnership channels through BDU and INSA); two
consecutive sprint milestones missed (build-delivery risk — descope to
the MVP core or bring in contractor capacity within two weeks); any
pilot customer disengaging from the steering-committee rhythm
(conversion risk — founder-level intervention); analyst utilisation
above 85 percent for a full month (SOC staffing risk — begin hiring);
and any grant rejection citing institutional capacity (governance signal
— accelerate advisory-board formation). Indicators are cheap to watch
and expensive to ignore; the monthly review should take one hour and
follow the register's order.

14. Consolidated Strategic Recommendations

The ten recommendations below synthesise the roadmap. Each is stated
with its rationale and horizon: immediate (within 30 days), near-term
(within 6 months), or structural (within 12 months). They are ordered by
priority, not by effort.

1.  Start the Phase 1 build now (immediate). Begin the six-month
    from-scratch build immediately: rule catalogue in Month 1, MVP and
    production deployment by Month 6. The build is the critical path
    everything else hangs from, and it is planned — five workstreams,
    hard exit criteria, tight MVP scope — so it can start the day this
    roadmap is adopted.

2.  Convert claims to commitments (immediate). Replace every “Planned”
    pilot with a signed letter of intent before the proposal is shown
    externally again. One signed bank LOI changes the document's status
    from promising to credible.

3.  Establish the legal entity and the BDU agreement (immediate).
    Register the PLC and execute a technology-transfer agreement
    covering IP, students, facilities, and ethics. This unlocks
    contracts, grants, and investment — none of which can flow to an
    informal lab.

4.  Re-sequence into three tracks (immediate). Run Phases 1–2 as the
    commercial core, Phase 3 as the grant-funded mission track, and
    Phases 4–5 behind explicit partnership, authorisation, and
    human-rights gates. Focus is the scarcest resource a nine-person
    company has.

5.  Charge for pilots (near-term). Six-month paid pilots at a defined
    discount, never free engagements, contracted against the MVP scope
    for a Month 6 start. Paid pilots select committed customers, fund
    delivery, and produce the reference stories that sell the next ten.

6.  Adopt USD-indexed or annually adjusted pricing (near-term).
    Multi-year contracts priced only in birr transfer depreciation risk
    entirely onto the venture. Pricing discipline set at the first
    contract is nearly impossible to retrofit.

7.  Pursue ISO 27001 certification as a sales instrument (near-term).
    Certification is the standard answer to the bank-procurement
    question “who audits the auditor?” and, at indicative cost of USD
    15,000–30,000, is among the cheapest credibility assets available.

8.  Fund the first year with the hybrid stack (structural).
    Development-partner grants and university in-kind support first,
    pilot revenue second, equity last. Approach the funder families in
    Section 9 with the policy mapping already in the proposal's
    appendices.

9.  Gate Phases 4 and 5 behind human-rights and institutional criteria
    (structural). No ACFS deployment without the EMA partnership,
    EHRC-engaged due diligence, and the proposal's own transparency
    framework verbatim; no CII monitoring without operator MOUs,
    confirmed authorisation, and insurance. The gates protect everything
    else the venture is building.

10. De-risk the founder (structural). Hire the CTO early, document
    systems and decisions, stand up the advisory board, and let the
    university agreement carry institutional continuity. Investors price
    key-person risk; this roadmap systematically removes it.

Bahir Dar University's invitation to anchor Ethiopia's digital
sovereignty is well founded: the research design is rigorous, the policy
alignment is direct, and the plan in this roadmap turns that design into
a working platform within six months of adoption. This roadmap adds the
discipline of sequence — build first, prove each ambition before
claiming the next, and rest every claim on a signed commitment or a
measured result. Ethiopia's climb from Tier 3 toward Tier 2 by 2028 can
be built on Ethiopian intelligence, Ethiopian systems, and Ethiopian
people.
